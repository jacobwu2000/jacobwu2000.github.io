# Theory or Observation? The *Epidemics* Constitutions and *Airs, Waters, Places*

<p class="lede">A close reading of the four “constitutions” of the Hippocratic <em>Epidemics</em> against the environmental theory of <em>Airs, Waters, Places</em>.</p>

<aside class="question-box">
  <p class="question-box__label">Working question</p>
  <p>
    Read closely against <em>Airs, Waters, Places</em>, do the constitutions
    (<em>katastaseis</em>) of <em>Epidemics</em> I and III look like its environmental
    theory <em>applied</em> to one place over time, or like the raw observation that
    such a theory could have been built from? And how do a few case histories sit
    inside the constitutions they belong to?

## Background

### The texts

**The *Epidemics*.** The Hippocratic Corpus includes seven books called *Epidemics*.
Books I and III are usually read as a pair [PLACEHOLDER: verify, and cite for the
grouping and date of Epid. I and III, e.g. Jones's introduction or Jouanna]. They
contain two kinds of writing. The **constitutions** (*katastaseis*) describe the
weather of one year, season by season, and then the diseases that occurred over
that year: which ones, in whom, and how they ran. The **case histories** follow
individual named patients, often day by day, to recovery or death. This project
reads the constitutions in W. H. S. Jones's Loeb translation, which divides them
into four:

- [Constitution 1](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-1) (Epid. I 1–3),
  [Constitution 2](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-2) (Epid. I 4–12)
  and [Constitution 3](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-3) (Epid. I
  13–26) each open “In Thasos”.
- [Constitution 4](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-4) (Epid. III
  2–16) opens “The year was southerly and rainy” and names no place.

The case histories in Epid. I come after Constitution 3. In Epid. III one
series comes before the constitution (Jones's section 1, before ch. 2) and another
after it [PLACEHOLDER: verify the number of cases in each series and where each case
is set, in the Loeb]. The constitutions also contain a few chapters of general method
rather than observation: Epid. I 11, I 23–26 and III 16. In the last of these,
the writer says one must “learn accurately each constitution of the seasons as well as
the disease” in order to “prognosticate” (III 16).

***Airs, Waters, Places*** (AWP) is a treatise about how environment shapes health.
It opens by telling the physician what to study: “what effects each season of the
year can produce”, the hot and cold winds, the properties of the waters, a town's
“position with respect to the winds and to the risings of the sun”, the soil, and
the inhabitants' way of life ([ch. 1](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/introduction#ch-1)).
The stated payoff is foreknowledge. A physician who knows these things will not,
“on arrival at a town with which he is unfamiliar, be ignorant of the local
diseases”, and “as time and the year passes he will be able to tell what epidemic
diseases will attack the city either in summer or in winter”
([ch. 2](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/introduction#ch-2)). The
chapters this project uses fall into three groups:

- [Airs](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/airs) (ch. 3–6): cities facing
  each direction and their prevailing winds.
- [Waters](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/waters) (ch. 7–9): kinds of
  drinking water.
- [Seasons](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/seasons) (ch. 10–11): how
  the weather of the seasons, and departures from it, predict a healthy or unhealthy
  year.

The rest of the treatise, from ch. 12 (“So much for the changes of the seasons”),
compares the peoples of Asia and Europe and is not used here.

***Prognostic*** is a Hippocratic treatise on foreseeing the course and outcome of an
illness from the signs a patient shows [PLACEHOLDER: verify this description, and
that *Prognostic* is in Jones's Loeb vol. II (LCL 148), not vol. I; give its
chapter references once read]. It is not yet on this site.

### The problem

Scholars have traditionally assigned *Epidemics* I and III and *Airs, Waters,
Places* to the same medical tradition, often called “Coan” after the school
associated with Hippocrates on Kos [PLACEHOLDER: cite a source for the attribution
and for its limits]. Sharing a tradition, though, doesn't settle how the texts
relate. There are at least three relationships to explain:

1. **The *Epidemics* and AWP.** Are the observations in the *Epidemics* the kind
   of data that AWP's generalizations could have been built from, or are they
   examples written up in the light of principles like AWP's?
2. **The case histories and the constitutions.** How does the individual patient
   relate to the general picture of the year that the constitution draws?
3. ***Prognostic*.** Its interest in foreseeing outcomes bears on both of the
   above.

Scholars have taken a wide range of positions on these questions
[PLACEHOLDER: one or two sentences characterizing the range of positions, with
citations, once the scholarship below has been read]. This project takes up the
first two, and the third more briefly, for a small set of texts that can be read
in full.

## Sources and method

### Text and translation

All the passages come from W. H. S. Jones's English translation in the Loeb

(CC BY-SA 4.0). The text is copied by script, without hand editing, apart from a
few dashes the digital edition drops, which are restored from the Perseus reader. It has not

### How the texts were read

1. **Annotation.** Passages are highlighted by category: body systems
   on Airs and the constitutions, and weather “cases” on Seasons. A short
   synopsis is written for each page. The highlighting is shown on the text
   pages and records what each passage reports, before any comparison.
   [PLACEHOLDER: note which annotations are the author's own and which were drafted
   and then reviewed.]
2. **A claim list.** About 8–12 claims taken from AWP, each written as
   *condition → predicted tendency* in AWP's own words, and marked as either a
   firm causal claim or a looser correlation.
   [PLACEHOLDER: link to the claim list once it exists.]
3. **Comparison.** For each constitution: which claims' conditions it meets; whether
   what followed matches the prediction, partly matches it, contradicts it, or isn't
   mentioned; what the constitution reports that AWP doesn't account for; and any
   causal or generalizing language it uses. Every point is backed by quoted
   phrases. There are no tallies or statistics.
4. **Case histories.** A few cases are read against their own constitution, as
   illustrations.

### What would count as “evidence” or “illustration”

[PLACEHOLDER: confirm or revise these criteria **before** writing the comparison,
so the judgment below follows from criteria set in advance.]

- **Signs that the constitutions are closer to raw observation:** detail that no
  theory calls for; outcomes the writer couldn't explain or found surprising;
  results that go against AWP's predictions; little or no stated mechanism.
- **Signs that they are written in the light of a theory:** AWP's categories and
  vocabulary (winds as causes, phlegmatic and bilious types); explicit causal
  rules; summaries that present the year as an instance of a general type.

### What this study does not do

It doesn't identify the ancient diseases with modern ones. It doesn't code or count
the case histories, and it doesn't read the *Epidemics* or AWP beyond the passages
listed above.

## Limitations

- **A small corpus.** Four constitutions, a few case histories and part of one
  treatise. The judgment above is sized to that sample and is not a claim about the
  Hippocratic Corpus as a whole.
- **Translation.** The reading is done in English, in one translation (Jones, 1923),
  with only a few Greek terms spot-checked. Jones's choices of wording, and his
  division of the text into constitutions and chapters, shape what can be compared.
- **The digital text.** The passages come from a digital edition that has not yet
  been fully checked against the printed Loeb (see above).
- **No retrospective diagnosis.** The diseases are discussed in the texts' own terms.
  No attempt is made to identify them with modern conditions.

## Where the fieldwork fits

This research grew out of a month of fieldwork in Greece in summer 2026, at Kos,
Epidauros and other Asklepieia, and in Athens. The fieldwork is **context, not
evidence**, for the findings above. The places the constitutions describe, starting
with Thasos, were not visited, and nothing observed on the trip bears on how the
texts relate to each other. What the visits give is setting. Kos is the traditional home of
the tradition these texts are assigned to. The Asklepieia represent the other model
of healing, through the god and incubation, that Hippocratic medicine existed
alongside. The medical instruments in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens
show the material side of the physician's work. See the [Field Journal](/field-journal).
