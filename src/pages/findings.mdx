<p class="lede">A close reading of the four constitutions of Hippocrates’ <em>Epidemics</em> against the environmental theory of <em>Airs, Waters, Places</em>.</p>

## Summary

This project compares the four constitutions of *Epidemics* I and III, in Jones’s
translation, with the seasonal predictions of *Airs, Waters, Places* (AWP) 10. The
weather of each constitution came closest to the same AWP condition (a dry, northerly
summer followed by a rainy, southerly autumn), but the diseases AWP predicts for the
winter after it were matched poorly and unevenly, with scores from 0.00 to 0.60, and
the constitution whose weather fits that condition best matched none of them. The
constitutions also record many diseases and symptoms that AWP 1–11 does not predict,
and they give almost no mechanism linking weather to illness: their causal and
theoretical language is mostly about the course of an illness (crises, critical
days, relapses and signs). At the same time they share AWP’s framework: a year
described season by season against a norm, patients sorted by age, sex and humoral
type, and illnesses that turn at a crisis. The evidence leans toward reading the
constitutions as records made within a tradition that AWP also belongs to, rather
than as AWP’s predictions applied to a particular place, though this rests on a small
sample and, for the direct comparison, on a single condition in a single chapter of
AWP.

## 1. Background

### 1.1 Texts

***Epidemics*.** The Hippocratic Corpus includes seven books called *Epidemics*.
Books I and III are usually read as a pair. Jones, in a note at the opening of Book
III, writes that it “has always been regarded as a continuation of the first book.
Even a casual glance will convince any reader that the two books are really one
work.” [PLACEHOLDER: verify the page of this note in the printed Loeb, and add a
source for the date of Epid. I and III, e.g. Jones’s introduction or Jouanna.] They
contain two kinds of writing. The **constitutions** (*katastaseis*) describe the
weather of one year, season by season, and then the diseases that occurred over
that year: which ones, in whom, and how they ran. The **case histories** follow
individual named patients, often day by day, to recovery or death. This project
reads the constitutions in W. H. S. Jones’s Loeb translation, which divides them
into four:

- [Constitution 1](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-1) (Epid. I 1–3),
  [Constitution 2](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-2) (Epid. I 4–12)
  and [Constitution 3](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-3) (Epid. I
  13–26) each open “In Thasos”.
- [Constitution 4](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-4) (Epid. III
  2–16) opens “The year was southerly and rainy” and names no place.

Fourteen case histories follow Constitution 3 in Epid. I. Two of them are said to be
“In Thasos” (Cases IV and IX); the rest name no city, only local landmarks such as
“the wall” or “the temple of Heracles”. Epid. III has two series: twelve cases before
the constitution (Jones’s section 1, before ch. 2), one of them set in Thasos (Case
IV), and sixteen after it, set in Thasos (five cases), Abdera (six), Larisa (two),
Cyzicus and Meliboea (one each), with one naming no place. [PLACEHOLDER: verify these
counts and places against the printed Loeb; they were taken from the case headings and
opening sentences of the Perseus TEI.] The constitutions also contain a few chapters
of general method rather than observation: Epid. I 11, I 23–26 and III 16.

***Airs, Waters, Places*** (AWP) is a treatise about how environment shapes health.
It opens by telling the physician what to study: “what effects each season of the
year can produce”, the hot and cold winds, the properties of the waters, a town’s
“position with respect to the winds and to the risings of the sun”, the soil, and
the inhabitants’ way of life ([ch. 1](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/introduction#ch-1)).
The stated payoff is foreknowledge. A physician who knows these things will not,
“on arrival at a town with which he is unfamiliar, be ignorant of the local
diseases”, and “as time and the year passes he will be able to tell what epidemic
diseases will attack the city either in summer or in winter”
([ch. 2](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/introduction#ch-2)). The
chapters this project uses fall into three groups, which I have named as follows:

- [Airs](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/airs) (ch. 3–6): cities facing
  each direction and their prevailing winds.
- [Waters](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/waters) (ch. 7–9): kinds of
  drinking water.
- [Seasons](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/seasons) (ch. 10–11): how
  the weather of the seasons, and departures from it, predict a healthy or unhealthy
  year.

The rest of the treatise, from ch. 12 (“So much for the changes of the seasons”),
compares the peoples of Asia and Europe and is not used here.

***Prognostic*** is a Hippocratic treatise on foreseeing the course and outcome of an
illness from the signs a patient shows [PLACEHOLDER: verify this description, and
that *Prognostic* is in Jones’s Loeb vol. II (LCL 148), not vol. I; give its
chapter references once read]. It is not yet on this site, and work on its
relationship with the other two texts is yet to be done.

### 1.2 Problem

Scholars have traditionally assigned *Epidemics* I and III and *Airs, Waters,
Places* to the same medical tradition, often called “Coan” after the school
associated with Hippocrates on Kos [PLACEHOLDER: cite a source for the attribution
and for its limits]. Sharing a tradition, however, does not settle how the texts
relate. There are at least three relationships to explain:

1. **The *Epidemics* and AWP.** Are the observations in the *Epidemics* the kind
   of data that AWP’s generalizations could have been built from, or are they
   examples written up in the light of principles like AWP’s?
2. **The case histories and the constitutions.** How does the individual patient
   relate to the general picture of the year that the constitution draws?
3. ***Prognostic*.** Its interest in foreseeing outcomes bears on both of the
   above.

Scholars have taken a wide range of positions on these questions
[PLACEHOLDER: one or two sentences characterizing the range of positions, with

### 2.2 Comparability of AWP and the *Epidemics*

What a text records depends on what it is for, so the first step is to identify the
goals of each text.

AWP states its purpose in its introduction. It addresses a physician arriving “at a
town with which he is unfamiliar” and promises that he will “know beforehand the
nature of the year that is coming” and “be able to tell what epidemic diseases will
attack the city either in summer or in winter, as well as those peculiar to the
individual which are likely to occur through change in mode of life” (ch. 2). AWP
is therefore predictive: its goal is to *predict* disease from environmental
conditions before it happens. It also explicitly covers epidemic diseases, the
subject of the *Epidemics*.

The constitutions, by contrast, were written after a year had ended, by someone who
stayed in one place, and on their face they are observations of that year. Yet they
too look forward. Epid. I 11 tells the physician to “Declare the past, diagnose the
present, foretell the future”, and Epid. III 16 says one must “learn accurately each
constitution of the seasons as well as the disease” in order “to prognosticate
therefrom”. Retrospective as they are, the constitutions are meant to bear on future
cases. (Jones notes that III 16 “does not fit in with the context, and occurs again
at the beginning of the book” *On Crises* (*περὶ κρισίμων*), and that the editor
Ermerins brackets it, so it may not be the constitution writer’s own statement of
purpose; I 11 is not open to this doubt.)

Both texts therefore aim at *foreknowledge*, but of different things. AWP describes
general tendencies for any place that fits an environmental profile: its winds, its
waters, its seasons. The constitutions record one year’s weather and the diseases
that followed it, and unlike AWP they say nothing about the town’s aspect, its
waters or its soil.

This limits what can be compared. AWP’s introduction distinguishes “the local
diseases, or … those that commonly prevail” from the “epidemic diseases [that] will
attack the city either in summer or in winter” (ch. 2): endemic disease from
*epidemic* disease. Airs and Waters are about *endemic* disease, fixed by a town’s
orientation or its water. They mention epidemics only in passing: the inhabitants
“are liable to any epidemic disease that prevails through the change of the
seasons” (ch. 3; similarly ch. 4), and eye inflammations “are of short duration,
unless a general epidemic take place after a violent change” (ch. 3). Only Seasons
deals with disease that varies with the year’s weather, such as whether the autumn
was rainy or the summer dry.

The constitutions can therefore test only some of AWP’s claims. Three of them record
different years at the same place, and the fourth names no place, so location is
held constant and the claims about place in Airs and Waters cannot be tested. The
claims of Seasons can, and the comparison that follows concentrates on AWP 10–11.

### 2.3 “Evidence” vs. “Illustration” Hypotheses

The question is this: are the observations in the *Epidemics* the kind of data that
AWP’s generalizations could have been built from, or are they examples written up in
the light of principles like AWP’s? I call the first the **“evidence” hypothesis**:
the *Epidemics* would be the kind of underlying observation that led to the
tendencies AWP predicts. I call the second the **“illustration” hypothesis**: the
observations in the *Epidemics* would be illustrative examples of AWP’s predictions.
In other words, “evidence” supposes that the *Epidemics* informed AWP, and
“illustration” that AWP informed the *Epidemics*. The two are not mutually
exclusive; the texts may have informed each other.

I set the following criteria before a thorough comparison of the texts, to decide
which textual details point toward each hypothesis:

- **Signs that the *Epidemics* is closer to raw observation:**
  - Extraneous observations absent from any AWP prediction
  - A lack of mechanisms that explain the observations
- **Signs that the *Epidemics* was written in the light of AWP:**
  - Vocabulary and a selection of variables that mirror AWP
  - Observations that match AWP’s predictions well

### 2.6 Scoring the Constitutions

For each constitution, I identified the closest corresponding Seasons condition. Then,
for each numbered claim of that condition, I searched the constitution for a
corresponding observation, and compared it with the prediction from AWP 10 in four
categories:

1. **Disease.** Is the disease or health prediction from the AWP 10 claim present in
   any of the constitution’s observations?
2. **Patient.** If the AWP 10 claim limits its prediction to a specific demographic,
   is that correctly reflected in the constitution’s observation?
3. **Season.** If the AWP 10 claim limits its prediction to a specific season, is
   that correctly reflected in the constitution’s observation?
4. **Severity.** If the AWP 10 claim prescribes a degree of severity, or uses hedged
   language to adjust the confidence of its prediction, is that reflected in the
   constitution’s observation?

For each of the four categories, the Match was determined as either “Yes,” “No,” or “N/A.” A
Match of “N/A” was only given for a category if that category was not specified in the original
AWP prediction. For example, Constitution 1 observed that “few women” had hoarse voices in
early spring. The corresponding AWP prediction only notes that “hoarseness” is “likely” to occur
in the winter. Since the AWP prediction was not limited to a specific demographic, the additional
information provided by Constitution 1 that it was “few women” who experienced the
symptom was not penalized and instead received a Match of “N/A.” However, the confidence
level of “likely” in the AWP prediction was met with the modifier that only “few” women
experienced the symptom in the constitution, which was judged enough of a
discrepancy to warrant a “No.”

[PLACEHOLDER: verify this example. In Epid. I 1, “Few women were attacked” closes the

After determining the Match in each category, an overall Score between 0 and 1 was
calculated by dividing the number of “Yes” Matches by the number of Matches that were
not “N/A” (that is, those that were either “Yes” or “No”). A claim with no
corresponding observation at all scores 0, and a constitution’s Average Score is
taken over all the claims of its condition, including these.

The full comparison table for each constitution is at the end of its page, below the
annotated text. The results are in [3.1](#31-comparison-of-predictions-against-observations).

The Average Scores are scattered and inconsistent, running from 0.00 to 0.60. Every
constitution came closest to Condition E, which makes sense for the first three,
since they record the same place. Ironically, how closely a constitution’s weather
fits Condition E seems, if anything, to run opposite to its score. Constitution 3
fits Condition E best, yet none of E’s predictions is found in it. Constitution 4
fits only if a windless drought is read as E’s “dry, northerly summer”, yet it scores
highest. With four constitutions this is an observation, not a correlation.

The base for these results is narrow. Only Condition E was tested against all four
constitutions, and Conditions B and C only as alternatives for one constitution each.
Conditions A, D and F were not tested, so nothing can be said about them. What can
be said about the fit between observation and prediction applies, strictly, to
Condition E: five claims in two sentences of a single chapter (ch. 10) of AWP.

Within that limit, the poor match does not support the “illustration” hypothesis.
A year written up in the light of AWP would be expected to show the diseases AWP
predicts for weather of that kind, and these constitutions mostly do not. The poor
match is weaker support for the “evidence” hypothesis, though: if AWP’s
generalizations had been built from records like these, they would be expected to
agree with the records too. What the mismatch shows most clearly is that the
constitutions were not shaped to fit AWP 10, and that AWP 10’s Condition E was not
drawn from these particular years. That leaves the constitutions closer to raw
observation than to illustration.

Two further kinds of surplus are worth noting. First, the constitutions record what
did *not* happen: “in no case was there suppuration” (Epid. I 1); “No kidney
trouble” (I 5); “There was no bleeding from the nose, except very slight discharges
in a few cases, and no delirium” (I 6). They also admit where the writer could not
tell: “Coughs attended the fevers, but I cannot say that either harm or good resulted
from the coughing on this occasion” (I 7). Second, much of each constitution follows
the *course* of the illnesses rather than naming them: crises and their days,
relapses, coction, and the signs that foretold recovery or death (see 3.3.5). AWP
1–11 uses the word “crisis” only once and never mentions relapse.

Some of the constitutions’ observations do appear in Airs and Waters, but as the
endemic diseases of particular places: convulsions in children (Epid. I 6, 12; AWP
3), nosebleeds in young men (Epid. I 15; AWP 4), difficult childbirth (Epid. I 16;
AWP 4, 7) and strangury (Epid. I 5, 10; AWP 9). Since the constitutions never
describe Thasos’s aspect or waters, these can be counted neither as matches nor as
misses. The roles they play also differ: in the constitutions, nosebleeds and
strangury are often the *good* sign that ends an illness (“a proper hemorrhage
through the nostrils”, Epid. I 19; strangury “saved very many”, I 10). In AWP they
are simply diseases. The reverse surplus exists too: AWP predicts pleurisy,
pneumonia and catarrhs (ch. 3, 4, 7, 10), and none appears in the observation
chapters of the four constitutions.

The large number of extraneous observations points toward the “evidence”
hypothesis. One would not expect an illustration of a predictive theory to include so
many diseases, symptoms and outcomes outside the theory’s predictions, recorded
without comment or correction, or to note that something did not happen. This makes
more sense if the observations are raw, recorded material, which the writer would
have every reason to set down in full. Even a detailed record is selective, though,
and the constitutions’ selection, by season and by type of patient, is itself
discussed in 3.3.

### 3.3 Comparison of Vocabulary and Organization

#### 3.3.1 Patient Characteristics

AWP often uses humoral types (phlegmatic, bilious) to describe the patients who are
vulnerable to a particular disease, along with sex and age. In AWP 10 the fevers of
Condition B are “of the acutest type in all cases, especially among the phlegmatic”,
and dysenteries come upon “women and the most humid constitutions”; in Condition C,
“The bilious have dry ophthalmia” and “Old men have catarrhs”; in Condition D,
“phlegmatics and men over forty are likely to suffer from ardent fevers, bilious
people from pleurisy and pneumonia”. Airs sorts by sex and age in the same way:
“the women are unhealthy and subject to excessive fluxes”, “Children are liable to
convulsions and asthma”, those “more than fifty years old” are paralyzed by catarrhs
(ch. 3), and “Men under thirty suffer from violent bleedings at the nose in summer”
(ch. 4). Two things are typical of AWP. A type is often itself produced by the place
(“They must be bilious rather than phlegmatic”, ch. 4). And it usually comes with a
mechanism that explains why that type suffers: the bilious have dry ophthalmia
“because of the warm dryness of their flesh”, and old men have catarrhs “because of
their flabbiness and the wasting of their veins” (ch. 10).

The constitutions follow the same convention. They refer to age, “youths, young men,
and men in their prime” (Epid. I 1), name specific ages, such as patients who “were
about sixty years old” (Epid. III 4), and divide children finely: “those just
weaned, older children of eight or ten years, and those approaching puberty” (Epid.
I 10). They often refer to sex, especially to women and pregnant women (Epid. I 16),
and occasionally sort patients by humoral type: “Tenesmus affected young, phlegmatic
people; the chronic diarrhoea and acrid, greasy stools affected persons of a bilious
temperament” (Epid. III 14).

So AWP and the constitutions describe patients in related terms: humoral type, sex
and age. Shared categories of sex and age mean little on their own, since almost any
medical observation or prediction will say who was affected. The shared humoral types
suggest that both were written in the same tradition, which says nothing about
whether one text came before the other.

The constitutions do, however, describe patients in more detail than AWP, and by
characteristics AWP does not use: way of life, as in Constitution 1’s “usually those
who frequented the wrestling school and gymnasia” (Epid. I 1); appearance, as in
Constitution 4’s “physical characteristics of the consumptives” (“skin smooth,
whitish, lentil-coloured, reddish; bright eyes”, Epid. III 14); and, in Constitution
3, hair, eyes, voice and temperament, among those who died: “the smooth, the
fair-skinned, the straight-haired, the black-haired, the black-eyed … the thin-voiced,
the rough-voiced, the lispers, the passionate” (Epid. I 19). Constitution 4 also uses
types missing from AWP 10, “those with a melancholic or a rather sanguine complexion”
(Epid. III 14). Constitution 3 goes further and names individual patients, such as
“the slave of Erato and Myllus” (Epid. I 15) and “Philiscus and Epaminon and Silenus,
who died” (Epid. I 14). And where AWP explains why a type suffers, the constitutions
simply report which types fell ill.

Detail of this kind is unsurprising in a text meant to record what was seen, so on
its own it says little about the text’s relationship with AWP. If anything it fits
the observation reading slightly better: an illustration of AWP would have no need
for categories AWP does not use, and the absence of mechanisms matches the second of
the criteria for raw observation (2.3).

#### 3.3.2 Seasons and Weather

Both texts measure a year against a normal one. AWP 10 begins: “If the signs prove
normal when the stars set and rise… the year is likely to be very healthy” (Condition
A), and AWP 11 warns most of all against “the most violent changes of the seasons”.
Each constitution opens with a chapter describing the weather of each season of the
year: which way the winds blew, whether it was warm or cold, dry, humid or rainy.
The constitutions also judge the weather against what is usual. Constitution 2 opens
with “unseasonable wintry storms” and later notes that “the cold weather was not
exceptionally unseasonable” (Epid. I 4); Constitution 1 says “the winter was like a
spring” (Epid. I 1), much as AWP says a city facing the sunrise “is just like spring”
(ch. 5). Both texts also date the weather by the same star risings and settings
(Arcturus, the Pleiades, the Dog Star).

This similarity again reflects a shared tradition and method: disease is analysed
through the weather that came before it and may have affected the health of the
inhabitants. That alone does not imply a direct relationship between the two texts.
But the constitutions do each begin with the seasons and then arrange their
observations by season, which matches the approach of AWP 10, and this gives some
support to the “illustration” hypothesis. The support is limited, since anyone
keeping a record of a year’s weather and illness might arrange it by season. The
method chapters show, though, that the arrangement is deliberate (see 3.3.4).

#### 3.3.4 Causality and Explanations of Mechanism

AWP 10 gives mechanisms (“For whenever the great heat comes on suddenly while the
earth is soaked…”). The constitutions set out the weather first and the diseases
after. Whether that order is itself a causal claim, or just the format of a record,
is the question for this section.

Where the constitutions link weather to disease directly, they do it with a phrase
that summarizes the weather before the diseases begin: “The whole year having been
wet, cold and northerly, in the winter the public health in most respects was good”
(Epid. I 5); “The year having proved southerly, wet and mild, in the winter the
general health was good except for the consumptives” (Epid. III 2). In Jones’s
English these can be read as “because” or simply as “after”. Constitution 1 comes
closest to a stated cause, and the cause it names is the weather of the year before:
“early in the spring, as the previous constitution had proved the opposite and
northerly, a few patients suffered from ardent fevers” (Epid. I 1). [PLACEHOLDER:
spot-check the Greek of these three passages to see whether the construction is
causal or temporal.]

The one general causal rule about the seasons is in Constitution 4: “And it seems to
me natural that the coming on of summer should have been helpful. For the coming on
of winter resolves the diseases of summer, and the coming on of summer removes those
of winter. And yet in itself the summer in question was not healthful; in fact it was
suddenly hot, southerly, and calm. But nevertheless the change from the other
constitution proved beneficial” (Epid. III 15). Here the writer explains an
observation (fewest deaths in summer) by a general principle (“it seems to me
natural”), notices that the summer’s weather should by his own standards have been
unhealthy, and settles the conflict by appeal to the change of season. This is
theory applied to observation. But it is not AWP 10’s theory. A sudden heat after a
rainy, southerly spring is exactly the sequence of AWP’s Condition B, for which AWP
predicts a summer that “cannot fail to be feverladen”. Constitution 4 found the
opposite and explained it by a different rule.

The other causes the constitutions give are of two kinds, and neither is
environmental. Some explain an illness’s course from its signs: in Constitution 3
the writer infers from the many hemorrhages that “This humour, then, especially was
in great abundance” (Epid. I 15). Others explain individual exceptions by individual
circumstances: the daughter of Philo died although she had the favourable epistaxis
because she “dined rather unseasonably on the seventh day” (Epid. I 19); illnesses in
Constitution 2 were not dangerous “except for those who from all other causes were
predisposed to die” (Epid. I 6); erysipelas came “some from a known exciting cause and
some not” (Epid. III 3). Nowhere do the constitutions give a mechanism of the kind
AWP gives, linking a season’s heat, cold or moisture to a change in the body.

Yet the order of weather then disease is not merely a format. The method chapters
say that the weather matters. Among “the circumstances attending the diseases, from
which I framed my judgments”, Epid. I 23 lists “the constitution, both as a whole and
with respect to the parts, of the weather and of each region” and “the custom, mode
of life, practices and ages of each patient”. These are AWP’s own variables (AWP 1),
but in Epid. I 23 they are only two items in a long list that is mostly made up of
signs in the patient. Epid. III 16, if it belongs here (see 2.2), likewise asks for
knowledge of “each constitution of the seasons as well as the disease”.

The constitutions, then, seem to hold that weather and place bear on disease, without
saying how. They have a theory of relevance but not a theory of mechanism. On the
criteria set in 2.3, the lack of mechanisms points toward raw observation. The one
explicit seasonal rule, in Constitution 4, shows a writer willing to theorize, but
with a rule that is not AWP’s and that contradicts AWP 10’s prediction for the same
weather.

#### 3.3.5 Crises

The word appears at two scales. AWP 11 says crises come at turning points in the
year: “For it is especially at these times that diseases come to a crisis”. The
constitutions count crisis days within each illness (“When the exacerbations are on
even days, the crises are on even days”, Epid. I 26), and they rely on the idea of
coction (“Coctions signify nearness of crisis and sure recovery of health”, Epid. I
11).

The idea of crisis itself seems to be the same in both. In AWP 11, at a crisis
“Some prove fatal, some come to an end, all others change to another form and
another constitution”. Epid. I 26 gives the same three outcomes: “at these times
there will be the crises resulting in recovery, or death, or a tendency for better
or worse”. A crisis is the decisive turn of an illness, and both texts expect it at
predictable times. What differs is the clock. AWP times crises by the calendar: the
solstices, the equinoxes and the risings of the Dog Star, Arcturus and the Pleiades.
The constitutions time them by the illness’s own days: the even and odd days of Epid.
I 26, and the reckoning of intermissions and relapses in Epid. I 20–22.

The constitutions also connect the two clocks. In Constitution 3, “About the season
of Arcturus many had crisis on the eleventh day” (Epid. I 17); in winter the ardent
fevers and phrenitis went on, “but their crises changed” (Epid. I 22); and rigors at
the crisis were fewest in spring and “by far the most during winter” (Epid. I 22).
The season of the year changes the pattern of crises within each illness. Epid. III
16 states the link as a purpose: knowing “each constitution of the seasons as well as
the disease” makes it “easy to examine the order of the critical days, and to
prognosticate therefrom”.

So crisis looks like one idea used at two scales. The difference lies in what the
idea is used for. AWP 11 uses the crises of the year to warn the physician off
dangerous dates (“unless compelled one should neither purge, nor apply cautery or
knife”). The constitutions use the crises of each illness to foretell its outcome.
AWP 1–11 has none of the critical-day arithmetic, never mentions relapse, and uses
“coction” only for physical processes (the sun’s coction of rain water, ch. 8; the
bladder “concocts” urine, ch. 9), not as a sign in the patient. The theory most
visible in the constitutions is therefore about the course of an illness, with the
year’s weather as one input, rather than about the environment. That points away from
AWP and toward the prognostic interest of *Prognostic*.

### 3.5 Dating and Authorship

Whether the constitutions could be data for AWP, or illustrations of it, depends
partly on which text is earlier and whether the same writer or circle wrote both.

Each possibility limits what the argument can claim:

- **If AWP is earlier,** the constitutions cannot be the data it was built from,
  though earlier records like them could have been. The “illustration” hypothesis is
  then possible, and the poor match with AWP 10 (3.1) counts against it.
- **If *Epidemics* I and III are earlier,** they could in principle be among the
  material behind AWP. The poor match means at least that AWP 10’s season conditions
  were not drawn from these four years.
- **If they are roughly contemporary, or by the same writer or circle,** influence
  in both directions is possible, and the shared framework of 3.3 is easiest to
  explain. The question then becomes how one tradition’s records relate to its
  theory.

In the passages read, neither text refers to the other, so dating alone cannot
decide between the hypotheses.

## 4. Conclusion

### 4.1 Evidence or illustration?

On the criteria set out in 2.3, the evidence leans toward reading the constitutions
as closer to raw observation than to illustrations of AWP, with an important
qualification.

Three of the four criteria point that way. The constitutions’ observations match AWP
10’s predictions poorly (3.1). They record many diseases, symptoms and outcomes that
AWP does not predict, including negative findings and admissions of uncertainty
(3.2). And they give almost no mechanism linking weather to the body, while the one
seasonal rule they do state is not AWP’s and contradicts its prediction for the same
weather (3.3.4). The fourth criterion, shared vocabulary and variables, points the
other way: both texts describe a year season by season against a norm, date it by
the same stars, sort patients by age, sex and humoral type, and share the idea of
crisis (3.3). But that shared material is general rather than specific to AWP. What
is distinctive in AWP, its claims about place and its weather-to-body mechanisms,
is missing from the constitutions.

The answer may depend on which part of AWP is in question. Its claims about place
(Airs and Waters) cannot be tested against the constitutions at all (2.2), and its
claims about the seasons are, for Condition E, mostly not met. It may also depend on
the kind of theory. The constitutions are not theory-free, but the theory they use
most is about the course of an illness, crisis, coction and prognosis, with the
weather as one input among many (3.3.5). An environmental theory is present only as
the conviction that weather and place matter. At the level of the individual case
the question is still open (3.4).

This suggests an option beyond the two hypotheses: a common tradition that both texts
draw on. On this reading, the constitutions are records made within a framework of
assumptions (that seasons matter, that patients fall into types, that illnesses turn
at crises) which AWP also shares and which AWP turns into general predictions. The
constitutions do not look like AWP applied to Thasos. Whether records like them were
the raw material for AWP’s generalizations cannot be shown from this sample, and the
poor match suggests that, if they were, AWP 10 was not drawn from these four years.
This judgment rests on four constitutions, one condition of one chapter of AWP, and
a translation, and it is offered as a lean, not a verdict.

### 4.2 Limitations

- **A small corpus.** This project examines only four constitutions, a few case
  histories and part of one treatise. The judgment above is sized to that sample and
  is not a claim about the Hippocratic Corpus as a whole.
- **Scoring.** The choice of the closest condition, and each Yes, No or N/A, is one
  reader’s judgment. Only Condition E was tested against every constitution, and
  Conditions A, D and F were not tested at all (3.1).
- **Translation.** The reading is done in English, in one translation (Jones, 1923),
  with only a few Greek terms spot-checked. Jones’s choices of wording, and his
  division of the text into constitutions and chapters, directly shape what can be
  compared.
- **No retrospective diagnosis.** The diseases are discussed in the texts’ own terms.
  No attempt is made to identify them with modern conditions.
- **Lack of domain expertise.** The researcher of this project is not an expert in the
  Hippocratic Corpus, in ancient medicine or in Ancient Greek. This is the work of a
  single amateur and may not accurately represent current scholarly consensus or the
  opinion of an informed expert.
