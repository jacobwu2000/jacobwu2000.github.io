## 1. Background

### The texts

**The *Epidemics*.** The Hippocratic Corpus includes seven books called *Epidemics*.
Books I and III are usually read as a pair [PLACEHOLDER: verify, and cite for the
grouping and date of Epid. I and III, e.g. Jones's introduction or Jouanna]. They
contain two kinds of writing. The **constitutions** (*katastaseis*) describe the
weather of one year, season by season, and then the diseases that occurred over
that year: which ones, in whom, and how they ran. The **case histories** follow
individual named patients, often day by day, to recovery or death. This project
reads the constitutions in W. H. S. Jones's Loeb translation, which divides them
into four:

- [Constitution 1](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-1) (Epid. I 1–3),
  [Constitution 2](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-2) (Epid. I 4–12)
  and [Constitution 3](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-3) (Epid. I
  13–26) each open “In Thasos”.
- [Constitution 4](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/epidemics/constitution-4) (Epid. III
  2–16) opens “The year was southerly and rainy” and names no place.

The case histories in Epid. I come after Constitution 3. In Epid. III one
series comes before the constitution (Jones's section 1, before ch. 2) and another
after it [PLACEHOLDER: verify the number of cases in each series and where each case
is set, in the Loeb]. The constitutions also contain a few chapters of general method
rather than observation: Epid. I 11, I 23–26 and III 16. In the last of these,
the writer says one must “learn accurately each constitution of the seasons as well as
the disease” in order to “prognosticate” (III 16).

***Airs, Waters, Places*** (AWP) is a treatise about how environment shapes health.
It opens by telling the physician what to study: “what effects each season of the
year can produce”, the hot and cold winds, the properties of the waters, a town's
“position with respect to the winds and to the risings of the sun”, the soil, and
the inhabitants' way of life ([ch. 1](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/introduction#ch-1)).
The stated payoff is foreknowledge. A physician who knows these things will not,
“on arrival at a town with which he is unfamiliar, be ignorant of the local
diseases”, and “as time and the year passes he will be able to tell what epidemic
diseases will attack the city either in summer or in winter”
([ch. 2](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/introduction#ch-2)). The
chapters this project uses fall into three groups:

- [Airs](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/airs) (ch. 3–6): cities facing
  each direction and their prevailing winds.
- [Waters](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/waters) (ch. 7–9): kinds of
  drinking water.
- [Seasons](/sources/hippocratic-corpus/airs-waters-places/seasons) (ch. 10–11): how
  the weather of the seasons, and departures from it, predict a healthy or unhealthy
  year.

The rest of the treatise, from ch. 12 (“So much for the changes of the seasons”),
compares the peoples of Asia and Europe and is not used here.

***Prognostic*** is a Hippocratic treatise on foreseeing the course and outcome of an
illness from the signs a patient shows [PLACEHOLDER: verify this description, and
that *Prognostic* is in Jones's Loeb vol. II (LCL 148), not vol. I; give its
chapter references once read]. It is not yet on this site.

### The problem

Scholars have traditionally assigned *Epidemics* I and III and *Airs, Waters,
Places* to the same medical tradition, often called “Coan” after the school
associated with Hippocrates on Kos [PLACEHOLDER: cite a source for the attribution
and for its limits]. Sharing a tradition, though, doesn't settle how the texts
relate. There are at least three relationships to explain:

1. **The *Epidemics* and AWP.** Are the observations in the *Epidemics* the kind
   of data that AWP's generalizations could have been built from, or are they
   examples written up in the light of principles like AWP's?
2. **The case histories and the constitutions.** How does the individual patient
   relate to the general picture of the year that the constitution draws?
3. ***Prognostic*.** Its interest in foreseeing outcomes bears on both of the
   above.

Scholars have taken a wide range of positions on these questions
[PLACEHOLDER: one or two sentences characterizing the range of positions, with
citations, once the scholarship below has been read]. This project takes up the
first two, and the third more briefly, for a small set of texts that can be read
in full.

### Comparability of AWP and Epidemics

In order to discuss the relationship between AWP and *Epidemics*, it is important to first identify
the goals of each text. The intention or genre of a text has great bearing on what kinds of
information it discusses.

AWP’s stated purpose is in its introduction, addressing a physician “on arrival at a town with
which he is unfamiliar” and promises that they will be able to “know beforehand the nature of
the year that is coming.” The text also states that “he will be able to tell what epidemic diseases
will attack the city either in summer or in winter, as well as those peculiar to the individual
which are likely to occur through change in mode of life” (AWP 2). This tells that AWP is
predictive and forward-looking; its goal is to *predict* disease before it happens just by the
environmental conditions. It also explicitly covers epidemic diseases, which indicates a
relationship to the diseases observed in *Epidemics*.

The Constitutions from *Epidemics* were written after the end of a year, by someone who stayed
at a single location. Thus, the Constitutions may be treated as mere observations of a single year.
Epid. I 11 states that a physician must “Declare the past, diagnose the present, foretell the future”
and Epid. III 16 says that “With this knowledge it is easy to examine the order of the critical
days, and to prognosticate therefrom.” This indicates that the purpose of these texts, while being
retroactive observations, is meant to have bearings on future medical cases (e.g. the case
histories), and that they also have some sort of predictive power.

Thus, both AWP and the Constitutions seem to be aiming at *foreknowledge*, albeit
foreknowledge of slightly different things. AWP describes general trends in any given location
that fits a particular environmental profile, whether it be the winds, the waters, or the seasons; on
the other hand, Constitutions are observations of a single year’s environmental conditions and
the subsequent diseases that occurred. However, unlike AWP, they neglect to mention several key
characteristics of the location—the town’s aspect, its water conditions, its soil.

Particularly, AWP makes two kinds of claims. Again, this is illustrated by the introductory
chapters which distinguish “the local diseases, or… those that commonly prevail” from
“epidemic diseases will attack the city either in summer or in winter” (AWP 2). This is a crucial
distinction between *endemic* and *epidemic* disease that limits what can be compared between
AWP and *Epidemics*.

AWP’s Airs and Waters are specifically about the *endemic* diseases, fixed by a location’s
orientation or water characteristics. AWP 3 and 4, for example, only briefly mention that
“besides, [the inhabitants] are liable to any epidemic disease that prevails through the change of
the seasons.” There are only a couple of modifications to the disease predictions should an
epidemic occur, such as when it is claimed that inflammations “are of short duration, unless a
general epidemic take place after a violent change” (AWP 3).

Only AWP’s Seasons regards the disease that varies with the year’s weather, as it tracks general
health tendencies based on the characteristics of the seasons, e.g. whether the autumn was rainy
or the summer dry. As a result, Seasons is seemingly the only section of AWP fit for comparison
with the observations in *Epidemics*.

Thus, the Constitutions can only test a few specific claims. Since they record various years at a
single location, location is constant and thus cannot be tested. While it cannot test the location
claims from Airs or Waters, it is well-suited to testing the claims from Seasons. Much of the
comparison that follows will likewise concentrate on AWP 10-11.

### What would count as “evidence” or “illustration”

[PLACEHOLDER: confirm or revise these criteria **before** writing the comparison,
so the judgment below follows from criteria set in advance.]

- **Signs that the constitutions are closer to raw observation:** detail that no
  theory calls for; outcomes the writer couldn't explain or found surprising;
  results that go against AWP's predictions; little or no stated mechanism.
- **Signs that they are written in the light of a theory:** AWP's categories and
  vocabulary (winds as causes, phlegmatic and bilious types); explicit causal
  rules; summaries that present the year as an instance of a general type.

### What this study does not do

It doesn't identify the ancient diseases with modern ones. It doesn't code or count
the case histories, and it doesn't read the *Epidemics* or AWP beyond the passages
listed above.

## 3. Discussion and Analysis

### Constitutions

For each Constitution, I identified the closest corresponding Seasons condition. Then, based on
each numbered claim for that condition, I searched for any corresponding matches listed in the
Constitution. I compared the descriptions of the observations with the predictions from AWP 10
in four categories:

1. Disease. Is the disease or health prediction from the AWP 10 claim present in any of the
   Constitution observations?
2. Patient. If the AWP 10 claim limits its predictions to a specific demographic, is that
   correctly reflected in the Constitution observation?
3. Season. If the AWP 10 claim limits its predictions to a specific season, is that correctly
   reflected in the Constitution observation?
4. Severity. If the AWP 10 claim prescribes a degree of severity of its prediction or uses
   hedged language to adjust the confidence level of the prediction, is that reflected in the
   Constitution observation?

For each of the four categories, the Match was determined as either “Yes,” “No,” or “N/A.” A
Match of “N/A” was only given for a category if that category was not specified in the original
AWP prediction. For example, Constitution 1 observed that “few women” had hoarse voices in
early spring. The corresponding AWP prediction only notes that “hoarseness” is “likely” to occur
in the winter. Since the AWP prediction was not limited to a specific demographic, the additional
information provided by the Constitution 1 that it was “few women” who experienced the
symptoms was not punished and instead received a match of “N/A.” However, the confidence
level of “likely” in the AWP prediction was met with the modifier that only “few” women
experienced the symptom in the Constitution, which was determined to be enough of a
discrepancy to warrant a “No.”

After determining the Match in each category, an overall Score between 0 and 1 was calculated
by dividing the total Matches that were determined to be “Yes” by the total Matches that did
were not determined to be “N/A” (i.e. where determined to be either “Yes” or “No”).

### Time and Causality

How far apart in time are cause and effect, and is the cause ever stated? AWP's
conditions span two seasons and predict the next: the claims list's case E, for
example, runs from a dry summer and rainy autumn to the diseases of winter. Each
constitution runs through a whole year, and two of them look beyond it: Constitution
1 opens by referring to “the previous constitution” (Epid. I 1), and Constitution 4
says that “the change from the other constitution proved beneficial” (Epid. III 15).
AWP 10 gives mechanisms (“For whenever the great heat comes on suddenly while the
earth is soaked…”). The constitutions set out the weather first and the diseases
after. Whether that order is itself a causal claim or just the format of a record
is the question for this section.

[PLACEHOLDER: analysis. Where, if anywhere, do the constitutions state a cause, and
in what words? Constitution 4's general rule, “For the coming on of winter resolves
the diseases of summer, and the coming on of summer removes those of winter”
(Epid. III 15), is the main passage to consider.]

### Normal and Abnormal Seasons

Both texts measure a year against a normal one. AWP 10 begins: “If the signs prove
normal when the stars set and rise… the year is likely to be very healthy” (case A),
and AWP 11 warns most of all against “the most violent changes of the seasons”.
Constitution 2 opens with “unseasonable wintry storms” (Epid. I 4). Both texts also
date the weather by the same star risings and settings (Arcturus, the Pleiades, the
Dog Star) and describe it by wind direction and by wet and dry.

[PLACEHOLDER: analysis. Do the constitutions treat disease as following departures
from the normal and sudden change, as AWP does? Is the shared calendar and weather
vocabulary evidence of shared theory, or common Greek usage that both texts draw on
(check against a source before arguing either way)?]

### Patient Types

Who falls ill, and by what categories does each text sort people? AWP uses humoral
types (phlegmatic, bilious), sex, and age (“men over forty”, “old men”). Constitution
1 names a group by way of life: “usually those who frequented the wrestling school and
gymnasia” (Epid. I 1). Constitution 4 describes “the physical characteristics of the
consumptives”, and sorts other patients by type: “Tenesmus affected young,
phlegmatic people; the chronic diarrhoea and acrid, greasy stools affected persons of
a bilious temperament” (Epid. III 14).

[PLACEHOLDER: analysis. Do the constitutions use AWP's categories, and does that
differ between Book I and Book III?]

### Disease Surplus

Many disease names are shared (ardent fevers, dysenteries, ophthalmia, consumption,
quartans, dropsies). The constitutions also report diseases that nothing in the
claims list predicts. In Constitution 1, for example, “many had swellings beside one
ear, or both ears”, and “painful inflammations occurred either in one testicle or in
both” (Epid. I 1). They also record what did *not* happen: “in no case was there
suppuration” (Epid. I 1).

### Crisis

The word appears at two scales. AWP 11 says crises come at turning points in the
year: “For it is especially at these times that diseases come to a crisis”. The
constitutions count crisis days within each illness (“When the exacerbations are on
even days, the crises are on even days”, Epid. I 26), and they rely on the idea of
coction (“Coctions signify nearness of crisis and sure recovery of health”, Epid. I
11). *Prognostic* belongs in this section, since it too reads the signs and timing
of an illness to foresee its outcome.

### Limitations

- **A small corpus.** Four constitutions, a few case histories and part of one
  treatise. The judgment above is sized to that sample and is not a claim about the
  Hippocratic Corpus as a whole.
- **Translation.** The reading is done in English, in one translation (Jones, 1923),
  with only a few Greek terms spot-checked. Jones's choices of wording, and his
  division of the text into constitutions and chapters, shape what can be compared.
- **The digital text.** The passages come from a digital edition that has not yet
  been fully checked against the printed Loeb (see above).
- **No retrospective diagnosis.** The diseases are discussed in the texts' own terms.
  No attempt is made to identify them with modern conditions.

### Where the fieldwork fits

This research grew out of a month of fieldwork in Greece in summer 2026, at Kos,
Epidauros and other Asklepieia, and in Athens. The fieldwork is **context, not
evidence**, for the findings above. The places the constitutions describe, starting
with Thasos, were not visited, and nothing observed on the trip bears on how the
texts relate to each other. What the visits give is setting. Kos is the traditional home of
the tradition these texts are assigned to. The Asklepieia represent the other model
of healing, through the god and incubation, that Hippocratic medicine existed
alongside. The medical instruments in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens
show the material side of the physician's work. See the [Field Journal](/field-journal).
